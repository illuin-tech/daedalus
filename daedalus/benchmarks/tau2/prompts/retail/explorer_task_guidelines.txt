## What a good task looks like in this environment

Here the task is a customer's request, relayed to a support agent who must serve it within the
domain policy. What belongs here is what a real customer would say — a want, a complaint, a
question — not an internal work order.

**Leak nothing the agent could look up.** You have just spent turns discovering real order ids,
item ids, emails, prices and statuses. Rediscovering them is the work you are setting, so keep
them out of the request. A customer says "my last order", "the boots I bought last month", "the
cheaper one" — not an order id or an item sku. State a literal only when the customer genuinely
supplies it from outside: a new address they are giving you, the exact wording they want, a
budget they have decided. The test for every value: *could the agent find this by looking it
up?* If yes, take it out.

**Let difficulty come from the gap between what the customer says and what is allowed.** The
strongest requests are the ones whose naive, literal reading is WRONG — part of what is asked is
limited or forbidden by the policy, so the correct handling is a partial fulfilment, a
substitution, or a refusal with an explanation. Under-determined goals, one narrow correct target
among close alternatives, and choices that constrain each other are the other sources. Never
make difficulty out of the customer being rude, impatient, or changing their mind — a capable
agent absorbs that in a turn. And do not manufacture it by piling on more steps.

**Vary the genre deliberately** — over a run these should all appear:

- a request the agent must first work out the eligibility of — what is permitted, what it would
  cost, which variant applies — and then carry out only the permitted part;
- a single change carried out end to end and verified;
- a request touching several records or account-level settings that must stay consistent;
- a request whose correct handling under policy is a partial fulfilment;
- a request whose headline ask the policy forbids, where the correct handling is to decline that
  part and carry out the permitted alternative instead;
- a question, where what the customer wants is to be told something they cannot see themselves —
  what they were charged, what an item is made of, why the thing they asked for is not allowed.

A run of nothing but successful single changes has covered a fraction of what the agent meets. A
question is not a lesser task here: the attempt is scored on the database at the end AND on what the
agent said, so a request whose correct outcome is that nothing changed is perfectly gradable —
name the value the agent has to state, and it is checked. What does not work is a task with neither:
nothing changed and nothing checkable said.

**Write the customer, not just the request.** You are scripting someone who will be role-played
live, and how they behave decides how hard the task is. Two levers matter more than anything else
in the wording of the request:

*What they volunteer.* A customer who answers every question fully hands the work back to the
agent. Say how much they give up and when — "you answer what you are asked and nothing more", "you
do not mention the second order unless the agent asks whether there are others". This is the
difference between a task the agent must solve and a task the agent can simply ask its way out of:
if the customer is holding the answer and will recite it on request, there is no task left.

*How they react.* Give them at least one turn that fires on what the agent says or discovers, and
write it so it only fires then — a real customer does not pre-announce their fallback. "If the agent
tells you that variant is unavailable, you will take the other leather one." "If the agent asks you
to confirm, you change your mind and keep it." "You are only satisfied once the replacement is
actually reserved." Difficulty that lives in the customer's reactions cannot be short-circuited,
because the agent has to reach the branch before it learns the branch exists.
