## What a good task looks like in this environment

This environment is one person's digital life spread across apps — contacts and messages,
playlists and libraries, payments and shared balances, files, notes, todo lists. The requests
that belong here are the ordinary errands and questions that person would actually voice.

{{ goal_not_procedure("the person", "app", "solver") }}

**Leak nothing the solver could discover for itself.** This is the rule that matters most, and
the easiest to break: you have just spent many turns finding real ids, emails, paths, amounts,
dates and names, and it feels helpful to include them. It is not — rediscovering them is
precisely the work you are setting. Refer to things the way the person would:

- write "my Book Club group", not the group id;
- write "the friend who still owes me for dinner", not their email address;
- write "the receipt I saved earlier", not `/home/paul/documents/receipts/x.pdf`;
- write "since the start of the year", not a literal date;
- write "the most expensive one", not the amount.

The test for every value in your instruction: *could the solver find this by looking around the
environment?* If yes, take it out. State a literal only when the person genuinely supplies it
from outside — the exact title they want a new note to have, the wording of a message they want
sent, a price they have decided on. A task that survives this rule is one whose solution must
begin by searching and reading; that is the kind that teaches.

**Let difficulty come from scope, not from length.** A hard request here is one where the
person's words leave real work in deciding *what counts*: which records qualify, how far back
to look, whether there is more than one page of them, what to do when several match or none do.
That ambiguity is what a capable solver gets wrong. Chaining more steps, touching more apps, or
adding more required side effects only makes a task longer, and a long task is not a hard one.

**Vary the genre deliberately** — over a run these should all appear:

- a question with a factual answer (a count, a title, a name, a yes/no) that takes real digging
  to work out and is reported back rather than acted on;
- a sweep over everything that matches, where getting *every* item right is the point and
  missing one page or one record is failure;
- a single-app errand done thoroughly, in depth;
- an errand that genuinely needs two or three apps to reconcile something before acting.

None of these is more valuable than the others, and a run that produces only state-changing
multi-app workflows has covered a quarter of what the solver will meet.
